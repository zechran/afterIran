US–China Relations — Structural Accommodation Despite Iran

Summary: The Iran war has not derailed US–China relations — Beijing deliberately stayed out, and shared economic interests point toward a structural accommodation that could reshape global trade and supply chains.
Sources: GeoEcon-May2026.pdf (George Friedman analysis)  ·  Last updated: 2026-06-05

China's Deliberate Abstention

Despite widespread speculation, Beijing has stayed out of the Iran war entirely. China's criticism of the war was rhetorical; it assigned its ambassador in Pakistan to attend mediation talks in Islamabad rather than taking any material action. Rumors of Chinese military aid to Iran — largely emanating from Russia — have not been substantiated. The Trump–Xi summit was delayed from March 30 to May 14, not cancelled. (source: GeoEcon-May2026.pdf)

The reason for Beijing's abstention is twofold. First, China needs to rehabilitate economic ties to the U.S. — still its largest customer — as economic growth hovers below 5 percent. Second and more urgently, China is heavily dependent on oil and LNG imports passing through the Strait of Hormuz. Higher energy prices raise the cost of exports, which remain the primary engine of Chinese economic growth and public stability. China needs the war to end for both short-term domestic political reasons and long-term economic ones. (source: GeoEcon-May2026.pdf)

The Economic Strain on China

The Iran war has created instability through higher oil prices, supply chain disruptions and weaker global demand for exports — fundamentally threatening China's export-driven economy. Early strain is already visible: export growth dropped sharply from 21.8 percent in the first two months of 2026 to just 2.5 percent in March, partly due to higher logistical costs and disrupted shipping. Industrial output remains relatively strong, but consumption is weak — retail sales growth slowed to 1.7 percent in March. (source: GeoEcon-May2026.pdf)

The Case for Structural Accommodation

George Friedman argues that a deeper structural accommodation between the U.S. and China is not just possible but probable:

States don't have friends or enemies, just permanent interests. The U.S. was vital to China's initial economic explosion, and if China is to recover from its current malaise, the U.S. will be vital again. After World War II, the U.S. helped Germany and Japan evolve economically — not out of charity but out of self-interest.

Key signals pointing in this direction: China's vice premier and America's treasury secretary met in Paris after the war started, very likely discussing the agenda for the upcoming summit. A shared interest in reaching an understanding on trade and a muted reaction to the Iran war are indicators that a U.S.-China trade agreement is in the process of emerging. (source: GeoEcon-May2026.pdf)

Taiwan — Dual-Track Strategy

Tensions between China and Taiwan are evolving in form, not just intensity. Beijing is running a dual-track approach combining pressure with selective engagement:

Coercion: Multiple African nations denied overflight permissions for Taiwanese President Lai Ching-te's plane to visit Eswatini (Taiwan's last African diplomatic ally), after Beijing allegedly used debt-relief threats as leverage. This physically constrained Taiwan's ability to maintain diplomatic relations. (source: GeoEcon-May2026.pdf)

Engagement: China signaled willingness to resume direct flights and trade in specific sectors (aquaculture) following a visit by Taiwan's KMT opposition. Infrastructure proposals — including a bridge linking mainland China to the offshore Kinmen and Matsu islands — underscore symbolic and practical outreach. Discussions of a long-term cooperation mechanism between the KMT and the CCP suggest Beijing is attempting to influence Taiwan's internal political dynamics. (source: GeoEcon-May2026.pdf)

Meanwhile, Taiwanese businesses have dramatically reduced their exposure to mainland China — investment flows dropped from over 80 percent of outbound FDI in 2010 to under 4 percent in 2025. Production has relocated to Southeast Asia, Mexico, or back to Taiwan, while trade ties with the U.S. and West have deepened, particularly in semiconductors and AI. (source: GeoEcon-May2026.pdf)

What the Summit Could Mean

If the Trump–Xi summit lays the foundation for a structural deal, the implications are large: the U.S. and China together account for nearly half of the global economy. A new relationship based on mutual interest could fundamentally reshape global economic systems — particularly in AI, semiconductors, and energy. Corporate-level collaboration in the most dynamic sectors is a logical downstream outcome. (source: GeoEcon-May2026.pdf)

Other actors are watching closely. Japan and India would reassess regional security alignments. The EU would reconsider economic and strategic positioning. Russia would evaluate whether easing US–China tensions constrains or expands its own room to maneuver. (source: GeoEcon-May2026.pdf)

Investment Opportunities

US–China trade normalization plays — If the summit produces a structural framework, sectors most penalized by the tariff regime are positioned for re-rating: U.S. consumer goods exporters, Chinese EV and electronics manufacturers with U.S. market access ambitions, and cross-border technology licensing. The asymmetry is that markets are currently pricing in continued adversarial posture.

Taiwan supply chain diversification — still structurally valid — Regardless of diplomacy, Taiwanese FDI to China has fallen from 80% to under 4% — a structural shift that doesn't reverse. Manufacturing in Vietnam, Mexico, India, and Malaysia that absorbed this reallocation remains structurally sound as an investment thesis even under US–China détente.

Semiconductors and AI infrastructure — Any US–China accommodation creates space for partial technology re-engagement. Companies sitting at the intersection (TSMC, ASML, NVIDIA) would benefit from relaxed export control pressure, while domestic Chinese AI infrastructure (Huawei, SMIC) benefits from sustained domestic demand regardless of diplomatic outcome. See AI Talent Race.

Chinese domestic demand recovery — If accommodation reduces tariff drag on Chinese exports, the downstream effect is consumer income recovery in China. Sectors most sensitive: luxury goods, travel, premium consumer electronics, and domestic food companies importing soybeans and animal feed.

Watch: KMT vs. DPP dynamics in Taiwan — Beijing's engagement with the KMT opposition is a medium-term political risk for cross-strait stability. A KMT electoral gain in Taiwan would shift cross-strait dynamics significantly — toward engagement and away from confrontation — affecting risk premiums on Taiwan-exposed assets (TSMC, UMC, Foxconn supply chain).
