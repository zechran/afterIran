Strait of Hormuz — Energy and Fertilizer Choke Point

Summary: The Strait of Hormuz is the world's most critical maritime choke point — not only for energy but, critically, for fertilizer — creating a transmission chain from shipping disruption to food insecurity that materializes on a 6–18 month lag.

Sources: GeoEcon-May2026.pdf   Last updated: 2026-06-05
---

The Physical Constraint

Nearly 15 million barrels of crude oil per day and more than 112 billion cubic meters of LNG passed through the Strait of Hormuz in 2025 — approximately 20 percent of all globally exported oil and gas. The critical fact underpinning all analysis is that bypass capacity is structurally limited: alternate crude export routes handle only 3.5–5.5 million bpd, while spare Saudi and Emirati pipeline capacity adds roughly 2.6 million bpd. Even partial restrictions immediately raise costs system-wide; full bypass is not possible. (source: GeoEcon-May2026.pdf)

This vulnerability was compounded before the Iran war began. Houthi attacks starting in 2023 reduced Suez Canal tonnage by approximately 70 percent, forcing global carriers onto longer Cape of Good Hope routes — tightening vessel availability and raising freight costs before a single shot was fired in Iran. The global shipping system entered the Iran war with no redundancy. (source: GeoEcon-May2026.pdf)

The Fertilizer Dimension — The Underpriced Risk

The energy dimension is widely understood. The fertilizer dimension is not. The Gulf is the world's dominant regional exporter of the key agricultural inputs:

Around one-third of all global seaborne fertilizer trade transits Hormuz, with urea accounting for roughly two-thirds of Gulf fertilizer exports. DAP and urea prices were already spiking sharply by late April 2026. (source: GeoEcon-May2026.pdf)

The Transmission Chain

The mechanism connecting shipping disruption to food insecurity operates on a lag:

Hormuz disruption → fertilizer prices rise → production costs increase → farmer application rates fall → crop yields decline → food prices spike (late 2026 into 2027)

In the short term (weeks to months), the effects are inflationary: higher freight, insurance, fuel and fertilizer costs raise landed prices and pressure processors and retailers. In the medium term, if disruption persists through fertilizer procurement and planting windows, the problem becomes structural — lower yields in Africa, South Asia and Brazil creating tighter global supplies of wheat, rice, maize, soybeans and animal feed. The report explicitly states that governments still have a narrow window to prevent the current shock from becoming a broader food security crisis. (source: GeoEcon-May2026.pdf)

Insurance Costs as a Multiplier

War-risk insurance premiums surged by more than 1,000 percent in some cases. The risky maritime zone now extends beyond Hormuz into the Red Sea, the Gulf of Aden and the Arabian Sea simultaneously — meaning there is no single corridor that avoids elevated risk. This insurance shock directly raises the cost of shipping food, fertilizer and industrial inputs, with the cost passed through to importers globally. See Insurance Regionalization for the structural consequences. (source: GeoEcon-May2026.pdf)

Scenarios

Short-term (rapid de-escalation): Temporary price spikes; strategic reserves absorb the shock; inflationary but not structural. Biggest consequence is cost, not supply. (source: GeoEcon-May2026.pdf)

Medium-term (disruption persists through planting windows): Fertilizer procurement affected; yield declines in Africa, South Asia and Brazil; tighter global stocks of wheat, rice, maize, soybeans and sugar in late 2026 and into 2027. This is the scenario to watch. (source: GeoEcon-May2026.pdf)

Long-term (war continues through 2026): Structural changes — reserve building, supplier diversification, domestic fertilizer programs, coal-based nitrogen in Asia, permanently higher-cost global food chain. (source: GeoEcon-May2026.pdf)

Investment Opportunities

Fertilizer producers outside the Gulf — Nutrien (Canada), Mosaic (North America/Brazil), Yara (Norway) are price beneficiaries with supply continuity unaffected by Hormuz. Sustained elevated urea and DAP prices through at least one more planting cycle supports margin expansion.

Agricultural commodities — Elevated wheat, urea, DAP prices likely sustained through the 2026 planting cycle. Brazil soybean exposure is double-edged: record crop output but 70% fertilizer import dependency raises production costs, compressing margins even as global soybean prices rise. Sugar has an additional dynamic: higher Brazilian energy prices incentivize diversion of sugarcane to ethanol, tightening global sugar supply.

Alternative shipping route infrastructure — Cape of Good Hope rerouting has driven 30–40% traffic spikes at South and West African ports (Durban, Cape Town, Walvis Bay, Lagos). Port infrastructure, cold-chain logistics, and bunkering capacity in these corridors become strategic assets. Tanger Med (Morocco) also gains as Atlantic-Mediterranean hub.

Energy storage and microgrid companies — The bypass capacity gap cannot be closed quickly. Investment in distributed energy resilience is durable, not cyclical. Defense and industrial clients are both pulling in the same direction. See Defense Energy Resilience.

Short thesis — Gulf-dependent importers — Philippines, Bangladesh, Pakistan face structural fiscal pressure from simultaneous energy and fertilizer import dependence, currency weakness, and limited fiscal room. Sovereign debt of these countries warrants elevated risk premiums.

Watch: fertilizer procurement timing — The critical decision window is May–July 2026. If African, South Asian, and Brazilian farmers cannot secure affordable fertilizer for the 2026 planting season, the medium-term food scenario becomes base case by Q3 2026. Monitor DAP and urea forward curves as a leading indicator.
